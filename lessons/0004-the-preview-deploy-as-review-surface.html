Lesson 4 · Trunk-Based Development

The Preview Deploy as Your Review Surface

By the end of this lesson you'll treat each PR's preview URL — not just the diff — as where you actually review, and you'll have a repeatable checklist of what to do on that URL before you approve.

In Lesson 3 you learned to judge a PR's shape on sight. Now you open it. A reviewable PR gives you three things to look at — and the one most reviewers skip is the one that catches what code-reading can't: the change running, on its own live URL.

1Three signals, not one

Every PR onto your protected trunk arrives with three independent signals. Approving means all three are green — and they tell you different things:

✓ CI checks
It builds and the tests pass. Proves the code is internally consistent — not that it does the right thing.
✓ The diff
You read how it's built (Lesson 3's S·S·S). Proves the approach is sound — not that it works for a user.
✓ The preview deploy
You drive the change running. Proves it actually behaves — the half the other two can't show you.
Why this matters double with AI committers: an agent's diff can look clean and its tests can pass while the actual experience is wrong — a button that does nothing, a broken empty state, a layout that collapses on mobile. Tests check what someone thought to assert; the preview shows you what's really there. For work you're personally signing off on, "I read the diff" is not the same as "I saw it work."

2What a preview deploy actually is

On a Vercel/Netlify-style host, you don't set this up per PR — it's automatic. Vercel creates a preview deployment whenever you push to any non-production branch or open a PR.1 Each one is:

  • Its own live URL. A real, running copy of the whole app with this PR's changes — not your machine, not production. The branch URL always points to the branch's latest commit, so it updates on every push.1
  • Isolated from production. A separate environment with its own environment variables (e.g. a test database), so nothing you click can touch real users or real data.1
  • Linked right on the PR. The host posts the preview URL and its build status as a comment/check on the GitHub PR, so it's one click from the diff you're already reading.1

3What to actually do on the preview URL

Open the preview and work this list. It's short on purpose — you're confirming behavior, not auditing the whole app. Tick each as you reason through it:

Preview review checklist

Does the thing the PR claims to do?Use the description's "what to check" line (Lesson 3's self-describing property). Walk that exact path on the preview and confirm the stated outcome happens.
Happy path, end to endThe normal user flow for this feature works start to finish — not just the one screen in the screenshot.
Edge & empty & error statesEmpty list, bad input, the failure case. This is where agent-written UIs most often fall down, and where tests are thinnest.
Nothing obviously regressed nearbyClick the screens next to the change. A single-purpose PR (Lesson 3) makes "nearby" small — that's the payoff.

Your move as reviewer: the preview turns "approve / request changes" from a guess into an observation. If the preview is wrong, you request changes or send it back — you don't approve a clean diff whose behavior you never saw. Green CI + sound diff + a preview you actually drove = the bar for merging to a trunk you keep releasable.

4Practice: you've opened the preview

For each PR you've already triaged as reviewable, you open its preview URL and see the following. Decide: Approve & merge, or Request changes?

Add “forgot password” email endpoint
CI ✓ green · diff looks clean. On the preview: you submit your email at /login → “Forgot password”, get the “check your inbox” confirmation, the reset email arrives in the test inbox, and an unknown email shows a friendly “if that account exists…” message.
✅ Approve. All three signals are green and you saw the happy path, the email, and the empty/unknown case behave. That's a real review, not a guess.
New dashboard empty state
CI ✓ green · diff looks clean and small. On the preview: a brand-new account (no data yet) shows a blank white panel with a console error, instead of the “Add your first project” prompt the PR describes.
✅ Request changes. This is the whole lesson: CI passed and the diff read fine, but the running change is broken in exactly the case it was built for. Only the preview showed it. Never reachable from the diff alone.
Pricing page redesign
CI ✓ green · diff looks clean. On the preview: looks great on desktop, but at mobile width the three pricing cards overlap and the “Subscribe” button is pushed off-screen.
✅ Request changes. The conversion button is unreachable for mobile users — a real regression a desktop-only glance would miss. The 30-second mobile check on the preview is what caught it.

5Check yourself

1. A PR's CI is green and you've read the diff — it's small and the approach is sound. Why isn't that enough to approve?

Right. Three independent signals, three different proofs. The preview is where you see the running change — the part code-reading and tests can't guarantee.
Re-read §1. Tests only check what someone thought to assert, and a diff shows intent, not behavior. The third signal — driving the change on its preview URL — is what proves it actually works.

2. You click a PR's preview URL, sign up a fake account, and delete some records to test the empty state. Safe?

Exactly. That isolation is the point — the preview is a throwaway, production-separate environment, so you can poke at edge and error states freely.
Re-read §2. A preview deploy runs in its own isolated environment with its own env vars/data, separate from production. That's precisely why you can test destructive edge cases on it safely.

3. The preview for a small, clean-diff PR shows the feature's main button doing nothing when clicked. CI is green. What do you do?

Yes. The preview just earned its keep. Behavior beats a green checkmark; you withhold approval until the running change is right.
Re-read §3's reviewer note. The preview is your behavior signal — when it's wrong, you request changes. Approving broken behavior (or merging it "to fix later") puts a known-bad change on your releasable trunk.

4. Part of a PR is incomplete work hidden behind a feature flag. On the preview, what should you confirm about it?

Right. A flag lets unfinished work ride on trunk without exposing it. Your preview check is that it's genuinely hidden in the default state. (Full treatment in Lesson 6.)
Re-read checklist item 6. The whole point of a flag is that incomplete work can merge while staying hidden — so you confirm it's off by default on the preview, not that it's finished.
