Lesson 7 · Trunk-Based Development

When Production Breaks

By the end of this lesson you'll know that a "hotfix" isn't a special process but the roll-forward rule under a stopwatch — that an incident runs on two clocks (stop the bleed fast, fix the cause calmly), that your fastest mitigation is the flag kill switch you already built, why a revert is roll-forward and not a rollback, and the decision ladder for getting production healthy without making it worse.

This is the lesson where the last two pay off. Lesson 5 left you the roll-forward rule; Lesson 6 left you a kill switch. Now production is broken, users are hitting the bug right now, and your one job as release manager is to pick the move — fast, and without lighting a second fire while you fight the first.

1A hotfix is the roll-forward rule under a stopwatch

The instinct under pressure is to invent a special emergency process — a dedicated hotfix/ branch, an SSH session into the production box, a frantic rewrite shipped without review. Trunk-based development says: don't. A hotfix is the exact same trunk → production fix you already know from Lesson 5, just done fast.1 The skill isn't a new procedure — it's keeping your head and splitting the incident into two clocks that run at different speeds:

Mitigation
Seconds → minutes
Stop the bleed. Shrink the blast radius now. It doesn't have to be pretty, permanent, or even a code change — it just has to make users stop hitting the bug.
Remediation
Minutes → hours
Fix the cause. The real fix, on trunk, at a calm reviewed pace — through the same CI + diff + preview gate as any other change. No stopwatch on this one, because mitigation already bought you the time.

The classic hotfix disaster is conflating the two — hand-coding a root-cause fix under fire and shipping it unreviewed, which is exactly how a hotfix causes the second incident. Separate the clocks and the panic drains out: mitigate first to buy time, then remediate properly.

2Your fastest mitigation needs no code at all

Here's the moment Lesson 6's ops toggle earns its entire keep. If the thing that's broken is sitting behind a flag, your fastest, safest mitigation is to flip the flag off — seconds, no deploy, no PR, no reviewing-a-fix-while-the-building-burns.2 A kill switch turns the bug invisible to users at runtime, and it does it without touching the code at all.

That reframes the whole incident. The first question isn't "how fast can we ship a fix?" It's "can we stop the bleed without shipping anything?" When the answer is yes, you've converted a sev-1 fire-drill into a calm, reviewed fix you can land on Monday — because the users are already safe.

Your move as director: the very first question you ask in an incident is "is the broken thing behind a flag we can flip?" If it is, flip it, breathe, and move the root-cause fix onto Clock 2. This is the payoff you bought back in Lesson 6 when you put risky features behind a kill switch instead of shipping them naked — the insurance you hoped you'd never need is exactly what's saving you now.

3No flag? A revert is roll-forward, not a rollback

When there's no flag to flip, the next-fastest mitigation is to take the bad change back out. The trap — and it's the same one from Lesson 5 — is to "roll back" by redeploying yesterday's build. That makes the symptom vanish for a moment, but the bug is still on trunk: the very next merge re-deploys it, and you're back on fire.1

The trunk-based move is to revert on trunk. On GitHub that's literally the Revert button on the offending PR: it opens a new PR containing a revert of the merge commit, which you merge like any other change.3 History is preserved, nothing is patched sideways, and the undo flows trunk → production exactly like every other fix. A revert isn't a step backward in history — it's a new commit forward that happens to undo an old one. That's why it counts as roll-forward.

This is where small PRs cash out. A small, single-purpose PR (Lesson 3) reverts cleanly — one tidy commit comes out and trunk is healthy. A 2,000-line tangle that touched a dozen unrelated things does not revert cleanly; you can't pull one thread without unravelling the others. The reversibility you bought by keeping PRs small is never more valuable than at 2 a.m.4

4The decision ladder: get production healthy, fastest first

Put it together as a ladder. When production breaks, take the highest rung you can reach — they're ordered by how fast and how low-risk they are. The top two are mitigations (Clock 1); they buy the time to remediate calmly on the rungs below.

1
Flip the flag offfastest
The broken feature is behind a flag → kill-switch it. Seconds, no deploy, no code change. If you can reach this rung, you almost always should: it's the lowest-risk move there is.2
2
Revert on trunk
No flag, but you can point at the bad change → hit Revert on its PR and ship the revert. Minutes, and trunk is back to its last known-good state — through the normal pipeline, no sideways patching.3
3
Roll forward a fix
You can't simply remove the change (it's woven into shared code, or the bug predates any one PR) → fix it on trunk like a fast little feature and let the merge re-deploy. This is also where remediation lives after you've mitigated on rung 1 or 2.1
4
Cherry-pick to a release branch rare
Only if you owe the fix to a specific supported version you can't roll forward — the one Lesson 5 trigger. Fix on trunk first, then cherry-pick that commit onto the release branch — never the reverse.5
Your move as director: default to the highest rung available, and resist the urge to skip straight to rung 3 and hand-author a fix under fire. Rungs 1 and 2 cost almost nothing and make the users safe before anyone writes a line of code — which is the whole point of splitting the clocks. The rushed, unreviewed fix is the move that turns one incident into two.

5Reviewing under fire — and the branch you're not cutting

Even a hotfix PR gets all three review signals from Lesson 4 — CI, the diff, and the preview. The reason you can hold that bar under pressure is that you mitigated first: because the flag is off or the revert already shipped, the root-cause fix isn't racing a stopwatch, so it goes through the gate like anything else. For the fix PR itself, the discipline tightens to the S·S·S rubric (Lesson 3): the smallest possible diff, single purpose, and easy to revert — those properties matter most precisely when the stakes are highest.

And notice what you are not doing: spinning up a GitFlow-style hotfix/ branch off a production tag, fixing it in isolation, then merging it back into two long-lived branches. For a team that releases from trunk, that ceremony is pointless — trunk is production, so the fix just goes on trunk.1 The only time a separate branch re-enters the picture is the rung-4 supported-version cherry-pick — and even then the fix is born on trunk first.

Watch for these under pressure: a PR that asks to skip CI or the preview "because it's urgent"; an SSH patch straight to the running server; or a "rollback" that just redeploys an old build and leaves trunk broken. Each one either re-breaks on the next deploy or ships an unreviewed change into a fire. The answer is always the same shape: mitigate first (flag or revert), then remediate calmly through the normal gate.

6Practice A: what's your first move?

Production just broke. For each incident, pick the highest rung that gets users safe:

A newly-released recommendations panel is throwing errors for half your users. It's behind a flag you control.
✅ Flip the flag off. Rung 1 — seconds, no deploy, no code. Users are safe instantly; the real fix moves to Clock 2 and lands calmly later.
A PR merged an hour ago crashes the homepage. There's no flag, but the change is self-contained and can simply come back out.
✅ Revert on trunk. No flag to flip, but you can name the bad change — hit Revert on its PR and ship the new revert commit. Trunk is back to known-good in minutes.
Checkout is applying the wrong tax rate — a one-line bug deep in shared billing code that other working features also depend on. You can't just remove it.
✅ Roll forward a fix. No flag, and reverting would tear out shared code other features need. Fix the line on trunk like a fast little feature and let the merge re-deploy.
An enterprise customer pinned to release/2.0 for six months hits a bug that trunk (now on v2.4) already fixed. They can't take the upgrade.
✅ Cherry-pick to the release branch. The one rung-4 case: a supported version you can't roll forward. The fix already exists on trunk — copy that commit onto release/2.0, never the reverse.

7Practice B: which clock is it on?

Sort each action into the two clocks. Mitigation stops the bleed (fast, can be temporary); remediation fixes the cause (calm, reviewed):

Flip the broken feature's kill switch off so users stop hitting the bug right now.
✅ Mitigation — Clock 1. Stops the bleed in seconds without a code change. It's not the fix; it's what buys time for the fix.
Open and merge a revert of the bad PR to get trunk back to its last known-good state.
✅ Mitigation — Clock 1. A fast undo that contains the damage. The root cause still needs a real fix afterward.
Reproduce the bug with a failing test, fix the root cause on trunk, and ship it through the normal CI + preview review.
✅ Remediation — Clock 2. The real fix, done calmly through the full gate because mitigation already made users safe.
Harden the fragile input handling so this whole class of bug can't recur, as a normal reviewed PR.
✅ Remediation — Clock 2. Fixing the cause (and the class) at a calm pace. No stopwatch on this one — that's the point of splitting the clocks.

8Check yourself

1. In trunk-based development, a "hotfix" is best understood as…

Right. There's no separate hotfix procedure for a release-from-trunk team — trunk is production, so the fix goes on trunk like any other change, only quickly.
Re-read §1 and §5. A hotfix/ branch is GitFlow ceremony you're not adopting, and patching the server sideways gets undone by the next deploy. A hotfix is just roll-forward, fast.

2. Production is broken and the failing feature is behind a flag you control. Fastest safe move?

Exactly. The kill switch is rung 1 — seconds, no code, users safe instantly. That's the insurance you bought in Lesson 6 paying off.
Re-read §2. When the broken thing is behind a flag, the fastest, lowest-risk mitigation is to flip it off — no deploy, no reviewing-a-fix-under-fire. The real fix then moves to Clock 2.

3. There's no flag, and a recently-merged PR is clearly the culprit. To get trunk healthy fast, you…

Right. A revert is a new commit forward that undoes an old one — it flows trunk → production and preserves history. That's roll-forward, not a rollback.
Re-read §3. Redeploying an old build leaves the bug on trunk, so the next merge re-ships it; deleting history is neither safe nor necessary. Revert on trunk and let the revert deploy.

4. Why split an incident into "mitigation" and "remediation"?

Exactly. Mitigation (flag off / revert) makes users safe in seconds; that bought time lets remediation go through CI + diff + preview calmly. Conflating them is how a hotfix causes the second incident.
Re-read §1. The two clocks run at different speeds on purpose: mitigate fast to remove the pressure, then remediate properly through the full review gate. Urgency is the reason to keep the bar, not drop it.
