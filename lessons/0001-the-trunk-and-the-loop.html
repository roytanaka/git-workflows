Lesson 1 · Trunk-Based Development

The Trunk and the Loop

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to draw the one loop that is trunk-based development, explain why short branches beat long ones, and see exactly where you sit in it as the reviewer of AI-produced work.

Every Git workflow is really a story about one question: how do many in-progress changes become one shippable codebase without chaos? Trunk-based development answers it with the simplest possible shape — and almost everything else (releases, preview deploys, feature flags) hangs off that shape.

1The trunk

Picture a tree. The trunk is the one fat, continuous line running up the middle; branches are short shoots off it. In Git, the trunk is your single shared main line — the branch called main — and the rule that defines the whole workflow is this:

The trunk is always releasable.
At any second, you could deploy main to production and nothing would be broken or half-finished.

That one constraint forces every other habit. If main must always be deployable, then no one can park weeks of unfinished work directly on it — and no one can let a side branch drift so far that merging it back becomes a battle.1

2Why branches stay short

The enemy of a releasable trunk is the long-lived branch. The longer a branch lives away from main, the more the two drift apart — and the merge at the end becomes a painful, risky "big bang" where you reconcile weeks of divergence at once. You've felt the loose version of this: a branch you were scared to merge.

TBD's fix is almost aggressively simple: keep branches so short they can't drift. A change lives on its own branch only long enough to be built, reviewed, and merged — hours, not weeks — then the branch is deleted.2 Google's DORA research measured this and found it predicts higher delivery performance. Their markers of teams who do it well:

≤ 3active branches in the repo at once
< 1 daybranch lifetime — merge to trunk at least daily
0code freezes or "integration phases"

— DORA, Trunk-based development capability3

3The loop

Here is the entire workflow. One change makes one trip around this loop, and you run the loop again and again, many times a day:

The six steps:
  • ① Branch — cut a short-lived branch off main for one small, single-purpose change.
  • ② PR + CI — open a pull request; automated checks (build, tests) prove it doesn't break the trunk.4
  • ③ Preview — a live preview deployment is built automatically, with its own URL.5
  • ④ Review — a human looks at the diff and the running preview, and approves or requests changes.
  • ⑤ Merge — the change lands on main, which (because it was small and reviewed) stays releasable.
  • ⑥ Delete — the branch is deleted. It did its job; keeping it around just adds drift risk.
This is "scaled" TBD. A tiny team might skip the branch and commit straight to main. The moment changes flow through a branch + PR + review before merging, you're doing the scaled form.6 That's the form you want — because review is the entire point of your setup.

4Where you sit in the loop

Your setup has a twist most TBD writing doesn't cover: Claude Code does steps ① and ② — it cuts the branch, writes the code, and opens the PR. Automation does ② and ③. That leaves step ④, review, and step ⑤, the decision to merge, as your job.

Your role is reviewer + release manager. You are the human gate that protects "always releasable." The agent proposes; the preview deploy shows you the change running; you decide whether main absorbs it. Every later lesson — repo setup, PR sizing, releases, feature flags — exists to make that judgment faster and safer.

So when you direct the agent, you're really steering the loop: "make this change small enough that I can review one trip around the loop in a few minutes, and self-contained enough that merging it keeps main green." Branch lifetime and PR size aren't bureaucracy — they're the levers that keep your review tractable.
